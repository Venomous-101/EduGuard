TITLE: UK Department for Education Guidance on Generative AI in Education (2025)
SOURCE_URL: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/generative-artificial-intelligence-in-education

The UK Department for Education (DfE) sets out its position on generative AI in education in the policy paper "Generative artificial intelligence (AI) in education", which applies to England and was updated in June 2025. It covers educational technology tools powered by generative AI and large language models such as ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot. It is aimed primarily at schools and colleges, with a particular focus on compulsory education, though many of its considerations apply to other educational stages. The guidance is advisory rather than mandatory: it sets expectations and practical advice, not legal requirements, and schools retain discretion over adoption.

The DfE's position is broadly supportive of AI where it helps teaching and learning. It presents generative AI as a tool that can reduce teacher workload, through uses such as lesson planning, formative assessment, drafting communications, and administrative tasks, and that can free teachers to spend more face-to-face time with pupils. Alongside the policy paper, the Department announced funding for AI tool development for marking and feedback, including an award of one million pounds to developers, and it supports the Oak National Academy's AI lesson assistant.

At the same time, the guidance sets clear expectations for safe and responsible use. Any use of generative AI by staff, students, and pupils should be carefully considered and assessed, weighing benefits and risks in the school's own context. Staff must critically review any AI-generated content before use, because tools can hallucinate, meaning they produce false or misleading information, reproduce bias from their training data, or mishandle sensitive data. AI should enhance teaching, not replace teacher judgment, and schools must take particular care with pupil-facing AI. The Department advises schools to use only approved tools and to align AI use with data protection and intellectual property law.

Data protection is a core requirement. Schools must ensure a lawful basis under UK GDPR for any personal data processed by AI tools, provide age-appropriate privacy information, minimize the personal data entered into AI systems, and consider sandboxed or restricted accounts. Keeping Children Safe in Education 2025 references the DfE's generative AI product safety expectations within its filtering and monitoring guidance, and treats misinformation and disinformation as safeguarding harms schools should be alert to.

In January 2025 the Department published "Generative AI: product safety expectations" (later renamed product safety standards), a non-statutory document addressed to edtech developers and suppliers, which schools can also use to assess AI products. It sets out the capabilities and features generative AI products should meet to be considered safe in educational settings: effectively and reliably preventing users from generating or accessing harmful and inappropriate content; maintaining robust activity logs; being secured against malicious use; prioritizing transparency and child safety in design and operating with accountability; and respecting data protection and intellectual property laws. The standards were updated in January 2026 to add expectations on cognitive development, emotional and social development, mental health, and manipulation.

In June 2025 the DfE also released a full support package, "Using AI in education settings: support materials", comprising training modules for teachers and leaders, leadership videos and transcripts on topics such as bias, data protection, and educational value, discussion materials for senior teams, and an audit tool to help schools assess their current position. Ofsted has published its own approach, stating that inspectors do not directly evaluate AI use but consider its impact on outcomes and experiences for children and learners, including whether risks around data protection, safeguarding, and bias were considered. The Joint Council for Qualifications provides separate guidance on AI misuse in assessments, including what counts as malpractice and detection requirements for teachers and exam centers.

TITLE: OECD AI Principles (2019, revised 2024) and OECD Guidance on AI in Education
SOURCE_URL: https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles

The OECD AI Principles are the first intergovernmental standard on artificial intelligence, adopted as an OECD Council Recommendation (OECD/LEGAL/0449) on 22 May 2019 and revised on 3 May 2024. They were endorsed by OECD member countries and have been adopted by additional adherent countries, forming the basis for the G20's AI principles. They are non-binding soft law, but they function as the default international reference for trustworthy AI, and they supplied the shared definition of an AI system and the AI life cycle framing that later instruments, including the EU AI Act, built upon.

The Principles have two halves. The first half sets out five values-based principles addressed to all AI actors: inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being, meaning AI should benefit people and the planet, reduce inequalities, and align with sustainable development objectives; respect for the rule of law, human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy, with appropriate safeguards and meaningful human oversight; transparency and explainability, requiring actors to provide meaningful, context-appropriate information so that people can understand and challenge AI outcomes; robustness, security and safety, requiring AI systems to function reliably and safely throughout their life cycle with risks continually assessed and managed; and accountability, holding the organizations and individuals that develop, deploy, or operate AI systems answerable for their proper functioning in line with the principles.

The 2024 revision was driven by the rise of general-purpose and generative AI. It sharpened the second principle's framing around human rights and democratic values, explicitly naming privacy, and added new emphasis on information integrity, including the risks of misinformation, disinformation, and synthetic content generated at scale. It also strengthened provisions on intellectual property, safety mechanisms such as the ability to override or decommission misbehaving systems, and environmental sustainability regarding AI's resource and energy footprint.

The second half contains five recommendations to governments: invest in AI research and development; foster an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem with digital infrastructure, data, and knowledge-sharing mechanisms; shape an enabling, interoperable governance and policy environment; build human capacity and prepare for labour-market transitions, including equipping people with AI skills; and cooperate internationally for trustworthy AI.

For education, the OECD's work goes beyond the Principles themselves. Through its Directorate for Education and Skills and the OECD.AI Policy Observatory, the OECD examines how AI reshapes teaching and learning, assessing both opportunities and risks. The relevant themes include: AI's potential to personalize learning and support teachers with administrative tasks; risks of bias, inequity, and widening digital divides when AI tools are deployed without safeguards; the need for transparency so that students and educators understand how AI-driven assessments and recommendations are produced; the importance of AI literacy and human capacity so that learners and teachers can critically evaluate AI outputs; and the requirement of meaningful human oversight over decisions affecting students' educational trajectories.

For a school-level AI policy, the OECD framework supplies several concrete expectations: deploy AI only where it demonstrably serves inclusive growth and learner well-being; keep humans in the loop on consequential decisions such as admissions, grading, and progression; disclose to students and staff when AI is being used and on what data it relies; manage safety and robustness risks across the tool's whole life cycle; and assign clear accountability for each AI system in use. The 2024 update's attention to information integrity is especially relevant to generative AI in classrooms, where misinformation and synthetic content are direct pedagogical risks.

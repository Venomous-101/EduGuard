TITLE: EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689): Provisions Relevant to Education
SOURCE_URL: https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj

The EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024 and is the world's first comprehensive horizontal law on artificial intelligence. It applies in phases, with different provisions taking effect at different dates. Although it is an EU regulation, its risk-tier framework has become a global reference for AI policy in schools and education systems worldwide.

The Act uses a four-tier risk model: prohibited (unacceptable risk), high risk, limited risk (subject to transparency obligations), and minimal risk. Education appears prominently in the two strictest tiers.

Prohibited practices relevant to schools are set out in Article 5 and have been in application since 2 February 2025. Article 5(1)(f) prohibits the placing on the market, putting into service, or use of AI systems intended to infer the emotions of natural persons in workplaces and education institutions. This covers systems that identify or infer emotional states such as anger, happiness, sadness, fear, surprise, or disgust from biometric data such as facial expressions, voice patterns, keystroke patterns, or body postures. The prohibition is motivated by the power imbalance between schools or employers and students or workers, and by the risk of discriminatory and intrusive outcomes. There are narrow exceptions for emotion recognition systems intended for medical or safety reasons, such as clinical monitoring or drowsiness detection. For schools, this means tools that claim to read students' emotions from faces or voices are banned outright.

Education is also listed as a high-risk use case in Annex III, point 3. The following AI uses in education and vocational training are classed as high risk: systems determining access or admission to educational institutions or assigning persons to education or training institutions; systems evaluating learning outcomes; systems assessing the appropriate level of education that individuals will receive or be able to access; and systems monitoring and detecting cheating behavior of students during tests, such as automated exam proctoring. The legislators justify this classification on the grounds that such systems may determine the educational and professional course of a person's life and affect their ability to secure a livelihood, and warn that improperly designed systems can be intrusive, violate the right to education and non-discrimination, and perpetuate historical patterns of discrimination against women, certain age groups, persons with disabilities, or persons of certain racial or ethnic origins or sexual orientation. High-risk AI systems are subject to strict obligations including risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record keeping, human oversight, accuracy and robustness, and conformity assessment, applying in a later phase of the Act's application timeline. A general study chatbot or lesson-planning tool that performs none of the listed Annex III functions is not high risk on that basis.

The Act also imposes transparency obligations in Article 50 that apply regardless of risk tier. Providers must inform users when they are interacting with an AI system; outputs consisting of synthetic audio, image, video, or text must be marked in a machine-readable format; and deployers of emotion recognition or biometric categorization systems must inform affected persons. Deepfakes must be disclosed. For schools, this means AI-generated content used in teaching or communications should be labeled, and students interacting with an AI tutor should know it is AI.

Providers of general-purpose AI models face dedicated obligations, including technical documentation and transparency about training data, with additional duties for models posing systemic risk. Schools deploying such models still carry the deployer duties above, including the AI literacy duty.

Article 4 requires providers and deployers of AI systems to ensure that persons operating and using the systems have a sufficient level of AI literacy, taking into account their technical knowledge and context. For education institutions, this translates into a legal expectation that staff who use AI tools understand how they work and their risks. Penalties for violations are severe, reaching up to 35 million euros or 7 percent of global annual turnover for the most serious breaches.

TITLE: UNESCO Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research (2023): Key Recommendations and Requirements
SOURCE_URL: https://news.un.org/en/story/2023/09/1140477

UNESCO published the Guidance for Generative AI in Education and Research in September 2023, describing it as the first global attempt to set standards for generative AI in education and research. The guidance was a response to the rapid spread of generative AI tools such as ChatGPT after their public release, and to the finding from a UNESCO global survey of more than 450 schools and universities that fewer than 10 percent of them had institutional policies or formal guidance on the use of generative AI applications, largely because national regulations did not exist.

The document builds on UNESCO's earlier work, including the 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence and the 2019 Beijing Consensus on Artificial Intelligence in Education. It promotes a human-centered vision grounded in human agency, inclusion, equity, gender equality, and cultural and linguistic diversity. UNESCO's Director-General stated at publication that generative AI can be a tremendous opportunity for human development but can also cause harm and prejudice, and that it cannot be integrated into education without public engagement and government safeguards.

The guidance is organized around a set of key steps for governments and institutions. It recommends that countries develop national policy frameworks and regulatory frameworks specific to generative AI in education and research, and issue guidelines covering the ethical, responsible, safe, transparent, and accountable use of these tools. It also urges countries to review and expand existing national AI strategies, noting that none of the strategies examined at the time covered generative AI as a specific issue, and to adopt data protection and privacy standards for the collection and processing of personal data by generative AI services. It further recommends revisiting copyright rules in light of generative AI outputs.

Data privacy is one of the central requirements. The guidance calls for legal frameworks that mandate the protection of data privacy and insists that educational institutions validate AI systems before allowing them into student learning. It sets out expectations for generative AI providers, including accountability for supporting ethical principles, protecting intellectual property, upholding ethical standards, and preventing the spread of misinformation and hate speech.

Age restrictions are a notable recommendation. The guidance calls on governments to consider an age limit of 13 for the use of generative AI tools in the classroom, aligned with the age thresholds used by many major providers. It suggests that teachers should not instruct students below this age in the use of generative AI, on the grounds that younger learners are more vulnerable to dependence on the tools and to harms such as distorted emotional and social development.

The guidance presents reasons for caution about uncritical adoption of generative AI in education. The concerns it highlights include the widening of digital divides, since access to generative AI architecture and training is concentrated in a small number of countries; bias in training data that reflects the values and norms of the Global North and underrepresents other cultures and languages; threats to academic integrity through plagiarism and cheating; risks of students becoming dependent on AI and losing opportunities to develop intellectual and cognitive skills; threats to teacher agency and the value of educators' professional practices; the spread of misinformation; and the lack of validation of AI systems before classroom deployment. It also flags that generative AI is being rolled out in schools quickly, without sufficient public scrutiny, checks, or regulations.

For practical action, the guidance recommends eight lines of action: ensuring connectivity and access for all; protecting human agency, including selective restriction of generative AI where it would deprive learners of cognitive development; monitoring and validating generative AI systems for education, with swift mitigation of inappropriate content; developing AI competencies and AI curricula for learners; building teacher and researcher capacity, noting that very few countries were investing in AI training for teachers; promoting plural opinions and critical evaluation of AI outputs; testing locally relevant application models and building an evidence base; and reviewing the long-term systemic implications of generative AI through cross-sector collaboration. It also stresses safeguarding the rights of teachers and researchers and protecting intellectual property as generative AI is integrated into research workflows.
